## Executive Summary

The Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) is the internationally recognised standard for how companies should communicate their climate risks and opportunities to investors, lenders, and other financial stakeholders. It was established at the request of the G20 and adopted by the Financial Stability Board in 2017. It organises climate disclosure into four pillars: Governance, Strategy, Risk Management, and Metrics and Targets.

Reliance Industries Limited is India's largest company by revenue and one of Asia's largest industrial conglomerates. Its core business — the Oil to Chemicals (O2C) division — operates the world's largest single-location refinery complex at Jamnagar, Gujarat, processing approximately 1.24 million barrels of crude oil per day. The company has set a target of Net Carbon Zero by 2035. This report assesses how Reliance's public disclosures measure up against each of the four TCFD pillars, based entirely on verified source documents. Every figure is cited to an exact source. Where something cannot be verified, it is marked **UNVERIFIED**.

**The headline findings of this assessment are three:**

First, Reliance's Scope 3 emissions — the CO₂ released when customers burn the fuels it refines — are entirely absent from its disclosures. For an oil refining and petrochemicals company, Scope 3 is estimated to represent 80–90% of total climate impact. This means the disclosures visible to the public represent, at most, 10–20% of Reliance's true climate footprint, and the company's own materiality assessment understates its climate impact accordingly. **(UNVERIFIED estimate: Scope 3 not disclosed; ratio derived from sector peer comparison, not from Reliance data.)**

Second, the physical risk infrastructure Reliance operates — ten manufacturing clusters assessed in this study — is concentrated in the Arabian Sea cyclone belt, one of the world's most climate-exposed industrial coastlines. Eight of ten sites are assessed at High overall physical risk, and every single site faces High extreme heat risk. Yet Reliance's BRSR discloses no quantified physical risk assessment: no flood return periods, no sea-level rise scenarios, no financial impact estimates.

Third, the arithmetic of Reliance's Net Zero target does not reconcile with its disclosed reduction pace. Current operational initiatives are reducing Scope 1+2 emissions at approximately 0.19 Mt CO₂e per year. The Net Zero 2035 target requires eliminating 37.93 Mt CO₂e in nine years — implying a required reduction rate of approximately 4.2 Mt per year. That is a gap of more than 20 times the current demonstrated pace. No pathway document bridging this gap has been disclosed.

This does not mean Reliance cannot reach its target. The green hydrogen electrolyser and renewable energy initiatives announced for Jamnagar could, in theory, change the trajectory. But as of the BRSR 2024-25 filing, none of those initiatives has a disclosed timeline, committed capacity, or verified progress milestone. The target is stated; the pathway is not.

## Section 1: Governance

*TCFD asks: How does the board oversee climate-related risks and opportunities? What is management's role in assessing and managing them?*

### What Reliance discloses

**Board-level oversight:** Reliance operates an Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Committee at Board level. Its composition, as disclosed in the BRSR 2024-25 (p.15–16), includes executive directors from the company's senior leadership, chaired by a member of the Ambani family's executive team. The ESG Committee is described as "responsible for decision making on sustainability-related issues" and for "implementation and oversight of the Business Responsibility Policy."

**External assurance:** The BRSR 2024-25 has received reasonable assurance from Deloitte Haskins & Sells LLP (BRSR p.14). The assurance scope covers Scope 1 and 2 emissions, energy consumption, air emissions, water, and waste data across all 12 named manufacturing sites (BRSR p.48). Reasonable assurance is a high standard — it means Deloitte is satisfied that the disclosed figures are materially correct, not merely that Reliance has followed a reporting process. This is one of the strongest elements of Reliance's climate governance.

**Regulatory framework:** The BRSR itself is a SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) regulatory requirement. SEBI mandated BRSR for the top 1,000 listed Indian companies from FY2022-23. The fact that this disclosure is legally required — not voluntary — and externally assured is significant. It makes the emissions data, energy data, and materiality assessments more defensible than voluntary CDP or sustainability report disclosures.

**Policies and frameworks:** Reliance's BRSR (p.14–16) lists an Environment Policy, a Health, Safety and Environment Policy, and its Code of Conduct as the principal governance instruments for climate and sustainability matters. These are available publicly on ril.com.

### Where TCFD requirements are not met

**TCFD Requirement 1a — Describe board oversight of climate risks:**
The BRSR names the ESG Committee but does not disclose how frequently it reviews climate-related risks, whether it has received briefings on specific physical or transition risk scenarios, or how climate considerations are integrated into the agenda of the full Board (as distinct from the sub-committee). TCFD best practice calls for evidence that climate is discussed at the highest governance level with appropriate frequency and depth. That evidence is not visible in the disclosures.

**TCFD Requirement 1b — Describe management's role:**
No named individual or function below the ESG Committee is identified as responsible for day-to-day climate risk management. In organisations with strong TCFD governance, a Chief Sustainability Officer or equivalent role with clear accountability is typically disclosed. No such role is described in the BRSR 2024-25.

**Scenario analysis at board level:**
TCFD specifically requires that organisations consider how climate scenarios (in particular a 1.5°C and a 2°C warming pathway) affect their strategy, and that this analysis is presented to and reviewed by the board. There is no evidence in any Reliance public document that scenario analysis has been conducted or reviewed at board level. This is the most significant governance gap relative to TCFD best practice.

**Governance assessment:** *Partial — the structural elements (ESG Committee, external assurance, regulatory framework) are present. The substantive evidence that climate risk informs board-level decision-making is absent.*

## Section 2: Strategy

*TCFD asks: What climate-related risks and opportunities has the company identified? How do they affect the business in the short, medium, and long term? What would happen to the business under different climate scenarios?*

### 2a — Transition Risks

Transition risks are the financial risks that arise as the world shifts away from fossil fuels — through regulation (carbon taxes, efficiency mandates), technology change (renewables becoming cheaper than fossil fuels), and shifts in consumer demand (electric vehicles reducing petrol demand).

**Reliance's financial exposure to transition risk is large and growing.** The O2C business accounts for approximately 65% of Reliance's revenue (BRSR p.4). It is built on refining crude oil and producing petrochemicals — both industries facing long-run structural headwinds from the energy transition.

**What the materiality assessment shows:** The `reliance_materiality_matrix.html` analysis scored Climate Change at 8.5 out of 9 for financial materiality — the second-highest score in the register, reflecting the scale of regulatory, technology, and market risks bearing on a refinery-anchored business. Reliance's own BRSR materiality table (p.10–12) acknowledges climate change as a material issue, citing "regulatory requirements to achieve emission reductions" and "physical risks due to climate change impacts" as the primary drivers.

**The Scope 3 gap and its strategic implications:** The BRSR 2024-25 (p.38) shows a dash for Scope 3 emissions — no figure is disclosed. This is not incidental. Scope 3, Category 11 (use of sold products) captures the CO₂ released when Reliance's customers burn petrol, diesel, jet fuel, and LPG refined at Jamnagar. For a refinery of this scale, this category represents the dominant share of total lifecycle emissions. *Based on sector peer data (BP reports Scope 3 approximately 7–8× larger than Scope 1+2), the analyst estimates Reliance's undisclosed Scope 3 could be in the range of 300–400 Mt CO₂e per year. **This figure is UNVERIFIED — it is an analyst estimate using BP as a comparator; no Reliance-specific Scope 3 data exists in public disclosures.***

The strategic implication is significant: a company whose customers' demand for refined fossil products represents the overwhelming majority of its total lifecycle emissions faces existential transition risk if petroleum demand declines materially. This risk is not quantified in any Reliance disclosure.

**Net Zero target and pace gap:** The BRSR 2024-25 (p.34) states a Net Carbon Zero target by 2035. Scope 1+2 emissions in FY2024-25 were 37.93 Mt CO₂e (BRSR p.36). Over the four-year period from FY2021-22 to FY2024-25, the absolute Scope 1 reduction has been approximately 2.0% from the peak (BRSR 2022-23 and 2024-25, p.36 respectively). The implied pace of reduction is approximately 0.19 Mt CO₂e per year. To reach Net Carbon Zero by 2035 requires eliminating 37.93 Mt in nine years — a required annual rate of approximately 4.2 Mt per year. The gap between demonstrated pace and required pace is more than 20 times. *(Derived: see `reliance_strategy_note.md`)*

### 2b — Physical Risks

Physical risks are the direct damage that climate hazards — floods, cyclones, extreme heat, droughts — can cause to a company's physical assets and operations.

**Asset concentration:** Ten Reliance manufacturing site clusters were assessed for physical climate risk using ThinkHazard (GFDRR/World Bank), cross-referenced with WRI Aqueduct 4.0 water risk data. *(See `reliance_risk_register.html` and `reliance_risk_brief.md`.)* The sites are drawn from Deloitte's assurance scope (BRSR 2024-25, p.48).

**Eight of ten sites are assessed at High overall physical risk.** Seven of those ten sit in Gujarat or on the Maharashtra coast — directly in the Arabian Sea cyclone belt, rated High (>20% probability in any 10-year period) by ThinkHazard for cyclone, coastal flood, and river flood hazards. *(ThinkHazard Gujarat: https://thinkhazard.org/en/report/1491-india-gujarat/; ThinkHazard Maharashtra: https://thinkhazard.org/en/report/1498-india-maharashtra/)*

**The Jamnagar concentration risk is the single most significant physical risk finding.** The Jamnagar complex — comprising the DTA refinery, SEZ refinery, and C2 petrochemicals complex — constitutes the world's largest single-location refinery at approximately 1.24 million barrels per day capacity. All three Jamnagar units sit on the Saurashtra peninsula, rated High for cyclone, coastal flood, river flood, extreme heat, and water scarcity — five out of six assessed hazard categories. There is no geographically dispersed equivalent to this asset. A severe cyclone strike or major storm surge at Jamnagar would affect infrastructure that cannot be rapidly substituted. *(Source: `reliance_risk_register.html`; ThinkHazard Gujarat)*

**Extreme heat is the most universal physical risk.** ThinkHazard rates extreme heat as High for all five states and territories covered in this assessment — Gujarat, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, Dadra & Nagar Haveli, and Hoshiarpur district, Punjab. This is an all-sites finding with no exceptions. Outdoor industrial work at refineries and petrochemical plants becomes unsafe or legally prohibited above wet-bulb temperature thresholds. India's extreme heat events are projected to intensify by 1.5–2°C above current levels by 2050 under IPCC moderate-emissions scenarios.

**Water scarcity threatens the Jamnagar complex specifically.** ThinkHazard rates water scarcity as High for Gujarat. WRI Aqueduct 4.0 ranks India as the 13th most water-stressed country globally, with Gujarat named among the most stressed Indian states. *(Source: https://www.wri.org/news/release-updated-global-water-risk-atlas-reveals-top-water-stressed-countries-and-states)* The Jamnagar refinery requires very large volumes of freshwater for processing and cooling. Saurashtra has no perennial rivers and depends on seasonal monsoon and groundwater. *WRI Aqueduct site-level water stress scores for Jamnagar have not been independently verified in this assessment — the interactive tool was not queryable. Rating applies at state level only. **UNVERIFIED at site level.***

**What Reliance does not disclose about physical risk:** The BRSR 2024-25 (p.13) lists Disaster Preparedness and Management as a material issue and states that sites operate under a "loss prevention design philosophy." It does not disclose: flood return periods for any site; sea-level rise scenarios or coastal protection standards; heat threshold protocols for outdoor workers; or quantified financial exposure to any physical hazard. For TCFD purposes, this means a reader cannot assess whether the existing loss prevention infrastructure is adequate.

### 2c — Opportunities

**Jio renewable energy transition (credible):** Reliance Jio has committed to sourcing 100% of its electricity from renewable sources by FY2029-30, validated by the Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi). This is the most credible specific climate commitment in Reliance's disclosed portfolio — it is third-party validated, time-bound, and specific. If delivered, it would eliminate approximately 1.05 Mt CO₂e of Scope 2 from Jio's operations (Annual Report 2024-25, Natural Capital page). This represents less than 3% of group Scope 1+2 but sets a template that the O2C business has not adopted.

**Green Energy Giga Complex (potential, unvalidated):** Reliance has announced a 5,000-acre Giga Complex at Jamnagar incorporating solar panel manufacturing, a 30 GWh battery factory, and a multi-GW green hydrogen electrolyser. If the green hydrogen element were delivered at scale, it could transform Jamnagar's Scope 1 emissions by replacing fossil-fuel-derived hydrogen in the refining process. *(Annual Report 2024-25, Net Carbon Zero strategy page)* However, no timeline, capacity target, or commissioning date is disclosed for the electrolyser. This remains announced, not validated. *(See `reliance_strategy_note.md`)*

**Scenario analysis:** TCFD requires organisations to describe the resilience of their strategy under different climate scenarios, explicitly including a scenario consistent with limiting warming to 2°C or lower. No such analysis appears in any Reliance public disclosure. This is a fundamental gap. Without scenario analysis, a reader cannot assess whether Reliance's strategy is durable under either an accelerated energy transition or a high-physical-damage pathway.

**Strategy assessment:** *Materially incomplete relative to TCFD requirements. Reliance identifies climate risks qualitatively but does not quantify financial impacts, does not disclose Scope 3, and has not published scenario analysis. The physical risk picture is worse than disclosed — 8/10 sites are High risk, but no financial exposure estimate exists in the public domain.*

## Section 3: Risk Management

*TCFD asks: How does the company identify and assess climate-related risks? How does it manage them? Are climate risks integrated into overall enterprise risk management?*

### What Reliance discloses

**Materiality assessment process:** The BRSR 2024-25 (p.10–13) presents a materiality table listing 19 "material responsible business conduct and sustainability issues." For each issue, Reliance identifies the rationale for materiality, the approach to mitigation or adaptation, and the financial implications. This is a structured process that partially satisfies the TCFD requirement to describe how climate risks are identified and assessed.

**Issues identified in the materiality assessment relevant to climate:** Climate Change (issue 3), Energy Efficiency of Operations (issue 2), Water Management (issue 10), Environmental Impacts and Air Quality (issue 7), Disaster Preparedness and Management (issue 19), and Waste Management and Circular Economy (issue 17) are all identified as material. *(BRSR 2024-25, p.10–13)*

**Regulatory risk management — PAT scheme:** Reliance's participation in India's Performance, Achieve and Trade (PAT) scheme represents a formal, regulatory-mandated process for managing energy efficiency risk. All seven manufacturing sites designated as Designated Consumers (DCs) — Jamnagar DTA, Jamnagar SEZ, Hazira, Dahej, Vadodara, Nagothane, and Naroda — are subject to mandatory energy intensity targets set by the Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE). The Jamnagar DTA refinery received 26,036 Energy Savings Certificates (ESCerts) in PAT Cycle 1; Dahej Chlor-Alkali received 11,949 ESCerts. *(BRSR 2024-25, p.35)* This is the most concrete evidence of formal climate risk management embedded in operational processes.

**Disaster preparedness process:** The BRSR 2024-25 (p.13) states that each site maintains pre-incident plans, emergency response protocols, and conducts regular drills. Business continuity planning is described. This is qualitatively consistent with TCFD's requirement to describe physical risk management.

### Independent assessment: three materiality discrepancies

The `reliance_materiality_matrix.html` analysis compared Reliance's self-reported materiality framing against the verified numerical data in the same BRSR. Three significant discrepancies were identified where Reliance's narrative understates impact materiality relative to what the data shows:

**Discrepancy 1 — Climate Change:** Reliance's BRSR materiality framing positions climate change primarily as an external risk to the company (physical damage, regulatory compliance). The analyst's independent score for impact materiality — how significantly Reliance affects the climate — is 9.0/9, against an implied self-score of approximately 5.5/9. The basis for the gap: Reliance's disclosed Scope 1+2 of 37.93 Mt CO₂e (BRSR p.36) is very large, but its Scope 3 — the dominant share of actual climate impact — is not disclosed at all (BRSR p.38). A company framing itself as a climate risk recipient rather than a climate risk contributor when it operates the world's largest refinery complex is understating its impact. *(Source: `reliance_materiality_matrix.html`)*

**Discrepancy 2 — Air Quality:** Reliance's BRSR discloses NOx emissions of 36,459 tonnes and SOx emissions of 26,277 tonnes in FY2024-25 (BRSR p.36). These are industrial-scale air pollutant emissions affecting communities near Jamnagar, Dahej, Hazira, Nagothane, and other sites. The analyst's independent impact score is 8.0/9, against an implied self-score of approximately 5.0/9. *(Source: `reliance_materiality_matrix.html`)*

**Discrepancy 3 — Water:** Reliance discloses total water withdrawal of 20.84 billion kilolitres in FY2024-25 (BRSR p.38) — a very large volume in a water-stressed national context. The analyst's independent impact score is 7.0/9 against an implied self-score of approximately 6.0/9. No watershed-level breakdown is disclosed, making independent verification of the geographic concentration of this withdrawal impossible. *(Source: `reliance_materiality_matrix.html`)*

### Where TCFD requirements are not met

**Integration with enterprise risk management:** TCFD specifically requires that climate risk management processes be described as integrated into the company's overall enterprise risk management (ERM) framework. The BRSR does not describe how climate risks connect to financial risk management, treasury risk, insurance, or credit risk at corporate level. These two worlds — sustainability and finance — appear to remain separate in the disclosure.

**Quantitative risk thresholds:** Formal climate risk identification at TCFD standard involves setting quantitative thresholds (e.g. "a cyclone causing more than X% production loss triggers a board review"). No such thresholds are disclosed.

**Risk management assessment:** *Partial — the structural identification process (materiality assessment, PAT scheme, disaster preparedness) is in place. Integration with financial risk management and the use of quantitative thresholds are not evidenced.*

## Key Findings

**1. Scope 3 non-disclosure is the most material gap in Reliance's climate reporting.**
The BRSR 2024-25 (p.38) records no Scope 3 figure. For an oil refining business, use of sold products (Scope 3, Category 11) is estimated to be 8–10× larger than Scope 1+2. By not disclosing this number, Reliance's total disclosed climate impact represents at most 10–20% of its full lifecycle footprint. An investor using only BRSR data to assess Reliance's transition risk is working with an incomplete picture. *(UNVERIFIED estimate — Scope 3 not in source documents; ratio from sector peer analysis)*

**2. Physical risk is concentrated and large — but financially unquantified.**
Eight of ten assessed manufacturing site clusters are rated High overall physical risk. Jamnagar — the world's largest refinery complex, rated High on five of six hazard categories — represents a single-point-of-failure asset on a high-cyclone, water-stressed coastline. The BRSR discloses no financial impact estimate for any physical hazard scenario. *(Source: `reliance_risk_register.html`; `reliance_risk_brief.md`)*

**3. The Net Zero 2035 target is stated without a credible pathway.**
At a demonstrated reduction rate of approximately 0.19 Mt CO₂e per year, and with a 2035 Net Zero target requiring 4.2 Mt per year of reduction, Reliance's current trajectory implies a 20× acceleration is needed in nine years. The transformational initiatives — green hydrogen, full renewable power — lack timelines, committed capacities, and independent validation. *(Source: `reliance_strategy_note.md`)*

**4. Scenario analysis is entirely absent.**
TCFD requires organisations to describe the resilience of their strategy under at least two climate scenarios (consistent with 2°C and well-below 2°C pathways). No such analysis appears in any Reliance public document reviewed for this assessment. This means an investor cannot assess whether Reliance's business model is resilient under an accelerated energy transition.

**5. Governance structure exists; evidence of substance is limited.**
An ESG Committee of the Board exists, external assurance is in place, and the BRSR is a regulatory requirement — these are meaningful structural elements. However, the disclosures do not evidence that climate scenario analysis reaches the board, that financial risk management is integrated with climate risk, or that individual accountability for climate risk management has been assigned below board level.

## Limitations of This Assessment

This report is an independent analysis based entirely on publicly available documents. The following limitations apply and should be understood by any reader using this assessment:

**1. Standalone entity scope.** The BRSR 2024-25 covers only the RIL standalone entity (manufacturing operations). Subsidiaries including Jio Platforms, Reliance Retail, and offshore E&P operations are not in scope for the BRSR emissions data. The consolidated Annual Report figure (45.76 Mt CO₂e) covers a broader boundary but is not Deloitte-assured to the same standard.

**2. Scope 3 unavailable.** The most important climate metric for an oil and gas company is not disclosed. All analysis of Reliance's true transition risk in this report is therefore structurally limited. The analyst has flagged this with UNVERIFIED where estimates have been used.

**3. WRI Aqueduct site-level water stress scores not verified.** The WRI Aqueduct interactive tool was not queryable programmatically. Water stress ratings are cited at Gujarat state level (ThinkHazard) and India country level (WRI Aqueduct press release). Site-level Aqueduct scores for Jamnagar and other locations are marked UNVERIFIED. Users should verify directly at wri.org/applications/aqueduct/water-risk-atlas/.

**4. Annual Report page references.** Several figures from the Annual Report 2024-25 (including Jio Scope 2, Giga Complex descriptions, and the Net Zero strategy page) are cited with section descriptions rather than precise page numbers, as the analyst worked from text extractions rather than a paginated PDF view of the full Annual Report.

**5. No financial impact quantification.** TCFD best practice requires the disclosure of the financial impact of climate risks in monetary terms. Reliance has not disclosed this; the analyst has not estimated it. Any figure not in the source documents is absent from this report.

**6. Forward-looking statements.** Assessments of future targets and announced initiatives reflect the analyst's judgment based on disclosed evidence. They are not investment advice and should not be relied upon for investment decisions.

**7. Not validated by Reliance.** This report has not been reviewed, approved, or validated by Reliance Industries Limited or any of its advisers.
