The World Bank's CO2 indicator (EN.GHG.CO2.MT.CE.AR5) is compiled using the AR5 methodology from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) and excludes emissions from Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) — meaning it captures what comes from energy, industry, and waste but not from deforestation or agriculture.

The renewable energy share indicator (EG.FEC.RNEW.ZS) is sourced by the World Bank from the International Energy Agency (IEA) and the UN's Sustainable Energy for All (SE4ALL) initiative. It includes traditional biomass (wood, charcoal, dung), which is why India's percentage appears high — a large share of rural households historically used biomass for cooking. As India modernised, total energy use grew faster than renewables, causing the percentage to fall even as the absolute amount of renewable energy increased.

## Key Findings

1. **India's economy grew more than five times over 20 years.** GDP rose from $709 billion in 2004 to $3.76 trillion in 2024 — one of the fastest sustained economic expansions in history.

2. **CO2 emissions nearly tripled, but per-person emissions remain low.** Total emissions grew from 1,168 Mt in 2004 to 2,807 Mt in 2022. Yet at 1.97 tonnes per person in 2022, India's per-capita figure is still roughly one-fifth of the US figure (~15 t) and about half the global average (~4.7 t). This is the core of India's position in international climate negotiations: the country emits a lot in total because it has a lot of people, but each person emits far less than in wealthy countries.

3. **Renewable energy share has declined — but not because renewables shrank.** The drop from 44.9% (2004) to 32.5% (2017) reflects the fact that overall energy demand grew faster than renewable supply during India's economic boom. Traditional biomass (firewood, crop residue) counted as renewable but was being replaced by fossil fuels in a growing economy. Since 2018, the share has stabilised as solar and wind expansion picks up pace.

4. **COVID-19 is visible in the data.** GDP fell in 2020 ($2.67 tn vs $2.84 tn in 2019), CO2 fell (2,357 Mt vs 2,551 Mt), and the renewable share ticked up — all consistent with an economy that temporarily contracted.

5. **India crossed the $1 trillion GDP threshold around 2007–2008**, a symbolic milestone in emerging market finance. It crossed $3 trillion in 2021 and is widely projected to be the world's third-largest economy by the early 2030s.

## Why This Matters for Climate Finance

India sits at the centre of the global climate finance debate for three reasons:

- **Scale:** With 1.45 billion people, any transition India makes (to clean energy, electric vehicles, green buildings) affects global emissions more than the same transition in most other countries.
- **Equity:** India argues it has the right to develop and lift people out of poverty, as wealthy nations did before worrying about emissions. Per-capita numbers make this argument concrete.
- **Opportunity:** India's clean energy market — solar, wind, green hydrogen, EV manufacturing — is one of the largest investment frontiers in the world. Billions of dollars of climate finance are being directed here.
